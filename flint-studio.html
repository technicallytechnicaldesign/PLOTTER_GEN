How the stone is built: each raw piece has an outline, a cortex (the weathered skin, stippled) and a toughness. A river cobble is smooth and keeps its cortex on the butt, a chalk nodule is lumpy with thick cortex and breaks with more steps and hinges, a tabular slab gives flat, thin bifaces, and a frost-fractured chunk has angular natural faces. The knapping method then removes flakes one after another. Each removal is a polygon struck from an edge or a platform, and a later scar cuts away whatever it overlaps, so the ridges fall where real ones do and are drawn with the 0.4 pen. Inside each scar the ripples are arcs centred on the point of percussion, drawn with the 0.3 pen, spaced wider as they move away from the blow, and clipped so that none crosses a ridge. A short line with ticks near the end of a scar marks a hinge or step termination. Views follow the usual conventions: long axis vertical, tip of a tool up, striking platform of a flake or core at the top, dorsal face first, then the other face (the ventral face of a flake shows the bulb of percussion, the eraillure scar, ripples and fissures), the platform, the side view and a cross-section. The blades and the Levallois flake are the very pieces removed from the core, so their dorsal faces show the scars that were already there. Cortex is stippled, denser toward the edge. Scale bars are in centimetres and the ratio is worked out from the actual drawing scale. On a reduction sheet the same piece is shown after each stage of work, with the flakes that came off. A plate lays out assorted finds at one scale, numbered, with a key. In Design Space: send all layers to Pen, black 0.4 for the key line, black 0.3 for the ripples and details, and a coloured pen for the cortex or the strike marks, then Attach and Make.

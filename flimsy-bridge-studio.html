Flimsy bridge studio

Card is a flimsy thing, so make it a bridge: six ways across a gap, each one a sheet that is plotted, cut out and folded into a truss. A small pin-jointed solver loads every one, and the pens draw what it finds: tension solid, compression dashed, the first member to go bowed or crossed, the sag drawn on the deck. A second plate keeps the one that really was flimsy. Day 13 of Inktober, "flimsy".

The card and the test

Stiffness, tearing, joint strength and the buckling knock-down are assumed numbers, not measured yet: pull and bend a strip of your card and put the real ones in. The order of the bridges is the part to trust, and "free" shows what a wobbly chord does to all of them.

Paper

Plot first with the split pen files (tension, compression, thin, black), then load the card back on the mat and cut the red file with the blue file scored. Every file carries four corner ticks so they land in the same place.

computing

How it is made: every bridge is a pin-jointed truss on centre lines, a pin at one foot and a roller at the other, loaded along the bottom chord and solved by the direct stiffness method. A member's force over its capacity gives the load the whole bridge can hold; a strut's capacity is the Euler buckling load of a thin card strip, so the long compressed diagonals of a Howe go before the long tensioned ones of a Pratt. The voids are the enclosed faces of the truss graph, shrunk by half a member width and cut; the plank is a bare strip of card with no truss at all. The deck of each net carries its own sag, drawn larger by the number printed on it.
